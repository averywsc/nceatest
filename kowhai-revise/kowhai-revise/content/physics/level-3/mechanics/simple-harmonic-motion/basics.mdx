## What is simple harmonic motion?

- <Term id="simple-harmonic-motion">Simple harmonic motion</Term> (SHM) is a back-and-forth motion where the **restoring force** is proportional to the displacement and points toward the <Term id="equilibrium-position">equilibrium position</Term>
  - mass on a spring: $F = -kx$
  - any SHM: $a = -\omega^2 x$
  - the minus sign means the acceleration always points back toward the centre
- Key quantities
  - <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> $A$: the largest displacement from equilibrium
  - <Term id="period">period</Term> $T$: time for one full cycle
  - <Term id="frequency">frequency</Term> $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$
  - <Term id="angular-frequency">angular frequency</Term> $\omega = 2\pi f = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}$

## Displacement, velocity and acceleration

- Start the motion at the right-hand end, then, with $\omega t$ in radians:

$$x = A\cos(\omega t) \qquad v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t) \qquad a = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t)$$

- Maximum values
  - **maximum speed** $v_{max} = A\omega$, at the equilibrium position
  - **maximum acceleration** $a_{max} = A\omega^2$, at the two ends of the swing
- At the ends the object is momentarily at rest, so $v = 0$ while $a$ is at its largest
- At equilibrium $a = 0$ and the speed is greatest
- The $a$ graph is an upside-down copy of the $x$ graph, because $a = -\omega^2 x$

## The reference circle

- Imagine a point moving at constant speed round a circle of radius $A$
- Its shadow on the horizontal axis moves in SHM with the same amplitude $A$ and the same period
- The angle of the point is the <Term id="phase">phase</Term>, and $\text{phase} = \omega t$
- Drag the point, or drag across any graph, to see how $x$, $v$ and $a$ change together

## Period formulas

- For a mass $m$ on a spring of stiffness $k$:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

- For a simple pendulum of length $L$ swinging through small angles (less than about 10 degrees):

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$$

- Neither period depends on the amplitude
- The pendulum period does not depend on the mass of the bob
- Doubling the mass on a spring multiplies the period by $\sqrt{2}$, not 2

<WorkedExample title="Mass on a spring">

**Problem.** A 0.50 kg mass is attached to a spring with stiffness 80 N m$^{-1}$. It is pulled 0.060 m from equilibrium and released.

<Parts>

1. Calculate the period of the oscillation.
2. Calculate the maximum speed of the mass.
3. Calculate the maximum acceleration of the mass.

</Parts>

#### Step 1: Period

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.50}{80}} = 0.497\ \text{s}$$

#### Step 2: Angular frequency, then maximum speed

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{80}{0.50}} = 12.6\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$$

$$v_{max} = A\omega = 0.060 \times 12.6 = 0.76\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$

#### Step 3: Maximum acceleration

$$a_{max} = A\omega^2 = 0.060 \times 160 = 9.6\ \text{m s}^{-2}$$

**Answer:** (a) 0.50 s (b) 0.76 m s$^{-1}$ (c) 9.6 m s$^{-2}$

<Mistake>Most common mistake: turning the fraction upside down and writing $T = 2\pi\sqrt{k/m}$. A stiffer spring must give a shorter period, so check that your answer goes the right way.</Mistake>

</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Pendulum length">

**Problem.** A simple pendulum has length 0.80 m. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.

<Parts>

1. Calculate its period.
2. Calculate its frequency.
3. What length would give a period of exactly 2.00 s?

</Parts>

#### Step 1: Period

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.80}{9.81}} = 1.79\ \text{s}$$

#### Step 2: Frequency

$$f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{1.79} = 0.557\ \text{Hz}$$

#### Step 3: Rearrange for the length

$$L = \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{9.81 \times 2.00^2}{4\pi^2} = 0.994\ \text{m}$$

**Answer:** (a) 1.79 s (b) 0.557 Hz (c) 0.994 m

<Mistake>Most common mistake: putting the bob's mass or the swing angle into the period formula. Neither appears, so any value for them in the question is a distraction.</Mistake>

</WorkedExample>

<Tips>

- State the direction of the acceleration when asked: it always points **toward the equilibrium position**.
- Put your calculator in **radian mode** before using $\sin(\omega t)$ or $\cos(\omega t)$.
- Gradient of an $x$–$t$ graph gives $v$. Gradient of a $v$–$t$ graph gives $a$. Say this when you explain a graph.
- Show the substitution into the formula, not just the answer, and give units.
- Convert centimetres to metres before you start.

</Tips>
